52 districts

This is California, split up into 52 congressional districts, with each one containing about 760,000 people. Hover over each district to view information about its political leaning and demographics. These districts determine California’s representation in the House of Representatives.

Gerrymandering vs Redistricting

Drawing districts is both complicated and common throughout the US and California, and is part of a process called redistricting. Normally, it happens once every ten years, after the census, because populations change as people move, are born, and die, and every district needs about the same number of people. Gerrymandering, on the other hand, is redistricting on purpose to favor one party. Mapmakers can do this by packing their opponents’ voters into a few districts, or cracking them across many so they fall short everywhere. In 2025, two states redrew their maps in the middle of the decade.

Why California redrew

California’s 2025 redraw was different because there wasn’t a new census. California redrew its map in 2025 in order to offset the effects of Texas Republicans reorganizing their map and adding up to five Republican seats to congress. To change the map, California needed its voters’ approval.

Proposition 50

Proposition 50 asked voters to temporarily replace the independent commission’s congressional map with a new map drawn by the legislature. It passed with 64.4% of the vote on Nov 4, 2025. Courts let it stand, and the commission returns after the 2030 census.

The 2021 map

Each color represents political lean; purple means leaned Harris, orange means leaned Trump. The outlined districts indicate which ones flipped from Trump to Harris after the 2025 redraw. Again, hover over each district to see specific data points/information about demographics and voter stance.

The 2025 map

Here’s the new 2025 map, currently being used for the first time in the Nov 3, 2026 midterms. Same voters, same votes: 47 of 52 districts now lean Harris, which is up from 41, and all six outlined districts flip their political leaning.

Shifting people, shifting power

Let’s observe one specific district: district 1. Under the old lines, the district leaned Trump 25 points, while the redrawn district now leaned Harris by 12 points. The district still has the same amount of people, but they do not have the same community of residents.

Same number, different place

With district 41, however, the striking difference lies in the district’s location. Crucially, none of the same territory is shared between the old district 41 and new district 41, which means if you lived in district 41 you would, no matter who you were, now reside in a different district.

Our district: CA-15

Now let's zoom in locally, to our very own district 15. Rep. Kevin Mullin’s district, shown here, is essentially identical to its pre-redistrict form post-redraw (leaning Harris by 48 points either way). The fact that nothing changed locally while major statewide shifts were occurring was really surprising to me.
